wrong in hospitality, it's rarely the decision that costs the venue — it's the missing

support-and-documentation layer, not a rates database.

Here's a pattern your readers will recognise: a venue dismisses someone for a genuinely
good reason — repeated no-shows, misconduct on the floor — and still loses the unfair
dismissal claim. Not because the decision was wrong, but because there was no written
warning, no documented process, no evidence of procedural fairness. A typical claim runs
$10,000–$50,000+ once legal fees land, and Fair Work penalties for non-compliance reach
$93,900 per contravention for companies.

The uncomfortable truth for hospitality: these decisions don't happen at 10am with HR in
the room. They happen mid-service, late at night, five minutes before open — made by an
owner or duty manager with no support, and nothing written down afterwards.

Three angles I can walk your readers through:

1. **The documentation gap.** Most small venues have no warning-letter trail, no
   performance records, and patchy employee records — the exact evidence Fair Work asks
   for first. (Employers are required to keep employee records for seven years and issue
   pay slips within one working day of payment.)
2. **Procedural fairness beats good reasons.** Claims are routinely lost on process —
   no warning, no chance to respond, no support person offered — even where the conduct
   justified dismissal.
3. **The 10pm decision problem.** The riskiest HR calls in hospitality are made
   after-hours under pressure, with no one to ask. What "having process in place"
   actually looks like for a venue with no HR team.
